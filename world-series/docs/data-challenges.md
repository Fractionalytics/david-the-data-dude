# Data challenges: world-series

What got in the way while building the World Series of Last Names, kept for the audience.

## Getting the data

1. **No single source has everything.** Baseball-Reference publishes every player's WAR as a bulk download but leaves out birthplace and position. MLB's own stats API has birthplace and position but no WAR. So the 2026 player table joins the two on MLB's player ID: 1,472 players.
2. **"On the 2026 list" isn't the same as "played in 2026."** MLB's 2026 player list has 1,511 names. 39 of them, Joe Musgrove among them, never appeared in a game (usually injured all year), so they have no WAR and are left out.

## Filling the gaps

3. **"Primary position" means two different things.** MLB's position label is a career tag. Recounting 2026 games by position moved 46 players. The biggest group was 16 listed shortstops who mostly played 2B or 3B this year. Catcher Maverick Handley only pinch-hit all year and kept his label.
4. **A dictionary for 1,190 surnames, with holes.** Wiktionary has a usable surname etymology for 929 of the 1,472 players. 537 others (Turang, Schlittler, Devers, Lindor, Tatis) have no entry, so Jev answers those from memory.
5. **Our own filter threw away good answers.** To skip empty entries, the first version required a minimum amount of etymology text, which dropped "Velázquez: from Velasco + -ez" because it was too short. Velázquez came back Toponymic until the filter was loosened. Then it was correctly Patronymic.

## Matching

6. **The same surname is spelled two ways.** MLB writes 8 players as "Rodríguez" and 6 as "Rodriguez", with and without the accent. Taking accents off, 1,192 surname spellings collapse to 1,177.
7. **Juniors and the Fourth.** 46 players carry a suffix (Jr., II, III, IV, V, Sr.), sometimes as part of the surname itself ("Acuña Jr."). It has to come off before classifying, or "Guerrero Jr." looks like a different name from "Guerrero."

## Classifying / judgment calls

8. **Latin American players officially have two surnames, but baseball uses one.** By Spanish naming custom, Pablo García Acosta takes García from his father and Acosta from his mother. MLB lists only the first, so the mother's half (and any compound name) is invisible for most of the 139 Dominican and 93 Venezuelan players. That understates both Compound and Matronymic for Latin America.
9. **Birth country is not nationality.** The data says where a player was born, not which passport he holds or where his family comes from. A Korean-American born in Texas counts as USA.
10. **The classifier knows Smith but not Burke.** In a 53-name pilot, Jev got the famous ones right (Smith and Cook occupational, Ohtani "big valley" toponymic) but called Burke patronymic (it comes from *burg*, a fortified place) and Warren patronymic (it comes from Varenne, a hamlet in Normandy). Jev is built to judge text it is given, not to remember etymologies. Handing it each name's Wiktionary entry fixed both.
11. **Lee is three different names.** English Lee means "meadow" (Toponymic). Chinese Li ("plum tree") and Korean Yi just come out as "Lee" in English letters. The dictionary's English entry was steering Hao-Yu Lee (Taiwan) and Jung Hoo Lee (Korea) to "meadow", so their players now get only Chinese or Korean entries. A first fix filed every Chinese and Korean surname under Other. That was dropped: these names do have origins (Kim means "gold"), they just don't fit European categories neatly. All 9 now rest on the classifier's own guesses, and none is good enough to make a roster. A US-born Lee could still be either.
12. **"Other" is mostly "we don't know."** 170 of the 199 players on Team Other have no dictionary entry. It's a mystery team more than a real surname type, and in the first pass it finished third.
13. **Shuffle the options and 9 in 100 answers change.** Reversing Jev's option list changed 9 of 100 answers, all of them ones Jev was less than 50% sure of (Wolfram flipped from Patronymic to Cognominal). 344 of 1,466 non-compound players fall below that line.
14. **The six hyphenated players needed a human.** Wiktionary had nothing on Falefa, Kiner, Shawver or Grauer. Kiner looks like a Jewish nickname for a violinist and Shawver a Flemish "market inspector", both low certainty. Falefa is a Samoan village ("four houses"), but nothing ties the family to it.
15. **Matronymic fielded two players.** Only Jackson Merrill (from Muriel) and Josue De Paula came back Matronymic, so it was folded into Patronymic ("named after a parent").
16. **Research only the names that could change the result.** Looking up all 537 unknown names by hand wasn't worth it, so we only researched uncertain players good enough to crack some team's lineup: 63 in the first round. That flipped 33 of them, including Bregman ("someone by the river bank") and Muncy (from a small hill in Normandy), both moving from Patronymic to Toponymic. Patronymic's lead shrank from 9.9 WAR to 0.8.
17. **The best surname dictionaries lock out robots.** Geneanet, Ancestry and forebears.io, which carry the Dictionary of American Family Names, refused every automated request (HTTP 403). Their entries were still readable in search-result snippets and on 23andMe's surname pages, which allow it.
18. **Francisco Lindor's name comes from an opera.** The research agents found what a manual Google search found: Lindor is mainly a Haitian and French West Indian surname, taken from the lovestruck hero of Beaumarchais's 1773 play *The Barber of Seville*. There's also a hamlet called Lindor in southwest France.
19. **The championship hung on two names our research couldn't trace.** After two rounds of research, Patronymic led Toponymic 68.68 to 67.85. Its rotation included Jesús Luzardo (6.49 WAR) and Jacob Misiorowski (6.46), which Jev had guessed were Patronymic at 16% and 34% confidence. A human Google search then found what the agents' search engine missed. Luzardo is popularly tied to *luz*, "light". Misiorowski's root *Misior* is a nickname word: "bear" by one account, "male mouse or rat" in the Polish Academy of Sciences surname dictionary. Both went to Cognominal, and Toponymic won 67.85 to 64.65.
20. **Every unknown name is a coin David can't flip.** Brice Turang (7.34 WAR) could have handed Patronymic the title if his name were patronymic, but nobody knows where it comes from. Its best lead is a family name from North Sulawesi, Indonesia. He stays on Team Other.
21. **Pete Crow-Armstrong nearly won it alone.** Crow and Armstrong are both nicknames, so he plays for Cognominal (David's rule: a compound name whose halves share a type joins that team). That lifts Cognominal to 67.79 WAR, 0.06 short of Toponymic's 67.85, less than one good inning. At that margin, reclassifying any one of 60+ top players would change the champion. Parker Messick is the weakest link: his name rests on one dictionary's "possibly from Messac, Brittany".
22. **A third of the classifications are educated guesses.** 1,002 of 1,472 players have a type backed by a dictionary entry, web research or a hand decision. The other 470 rest on Jev alone, Tatis and Chourio among them. Those guesses also drift: rewording one category's definition moved 51 of them into Other. The Latin America vs. USA comparison holds on the 1,002 known names: Toponymic 38% vs. 25%, Patronymic 43% vs. 41%.
23. **Born in Korea, named in Germany.** Rob Refsnyder was born in Seoul and adopted, so his birth country pointed the classifier at the wrong language. His name is most likely an American spelling of German *Reifschneider*, a hoop cutter or barrel maker.
24. **Looking up what the names mean reopened the race.** Writing a one-line meaning for each of the 52 players on the top four teams turned up three whose sources disagree with their team. Tarik Skubal is filed under Occupational, but his name is mainly a Czech nickname, "plucked". Ezequiel Duran and Henry Bolte come from first names (Durand, Baldo). Moving Skubal alone makes Cognominal champion, 68.13 to 67.85. Moving all three keeps Toponymic on top. Add "Messick unknown" to that and Patronymic wins, 66.27.
