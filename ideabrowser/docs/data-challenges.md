# Data challenges: ideabrowser

What got in the way of turning a year of newsletter emails into data.

## Getting the data

1. **Gmail only lets you read so fast.** Downloading all 427 Ideabrowser emails stopped after about 160, when Gmail hit its per-user limit on reads per minute. Pausing a third of a second between emails, and waiting a minute when the limit hit, got the rest. One more pause was still needed partway through.
2. **The newsletter changed shape halfway through.** From July 29, 2025 to July 27, 2026, the daily email was a structured "Idea of the Day" (352 emails). From July 28, 2026 on, it became a chatty newsletter with a headline-style subject ("Solo dining is surging"). Any comparison across the year has to deal with two formats, not one. The other 75 emails are the newsletter era (72) plus 3 onboarding emails sent in the first three days.
3. **A few days are simply missing.** 12 of the 436 days between the first and last email have no Ideabrowser email, including a 7-day run from November 16–23, 2025 (November 22 did arrive).
4. **One newsletter, three templates.** Ideabrowser redesigned its email twice: on January 26, 2026 ("New format today", the email itself says) and again on July 28, 2026. Each template puts the idea's title somewhere different, so the parser reads each era with its own rules. Through the first redesign, the one stable thing was the link to the idea's page (`ideabrowser.com/idea/<name>`), so that link is how ideas are identified, not their titles. The newsletter era drops those links entirely, so its 72 featured ideas have only a title.
5. **Sometimes the title just isn't there.** From May 29 to July 27, 2026 (56 emails), the "Idea of the Day" heading runs straight into the pitch paragraph with no title line, so the title comes from the subject line's short name instead ("Baby tracker you talk to"). And 75 of the 1,010 "more ideas" links (15 in era 1, 60 in era 2) are bare links with no title at all; only the name in the link describes them.
6. **The revenue promises come and go.** Ideas tagged with a dollar figure like "($10M+ ARR)" appear only before January 26, 2026, and even then on just 277 of the 692 ideas from that era (40%). After the redesign, no email title carries a revenue tag.

## Classifying / judgment calls

7. **The AI believed the example, not the product.** In the 50-idea pilot, a listing-sync dashboard for franchises was filed under Health & medical because its pitch opened with a dental group, and a "ChatGPT rank monitor" landed in Home services because the pitch mentioned roofers. One added rule ("if the pitch only uses one industry as an example, pick the job the tool does") moved 8 of the 50 industry answers; both of those became Marketing, sales & media.
8. **A title isn't a pitch.** About 1,000 of the 1,433 ideas arrive as a one-line title, with no description. To see what that costs, the 424 featured ideas were classified twice: once with their pitch, once from the title alone. The industry calls barely moved (83% agreement, and every industry's share within 3 points), but "Is AI central?" dropped from 47% Yes to 29% Yes on titles alone, because most AI products don't say "AI" in their name. So industry is measured on all ideas, while AI, who pays and business type are measured on the featured ideas only.
9. **Shuffling the answer choices didn't fool it.** AI classifiers can lean toward whichever option is listed first. Rerunning 100 ideas with the options in reverse order changed 1–3 answers per question (97–99% agreement), and none of the answers that were at least 80% confident.
10. **Everything looked like an "agent."** Asked whether each idea was one of Greg Isenberg's "vertical agents", the AI said yes to 303 of 424 featured ideas, including 127 it had separately judged not to be AI products, such as an online marketplace for burial plots. The fix combines two answers in code: an idea counts as an agent only if it's industry-specific *and* AI is central to it. That drops agents from 71% of ideas to 42%.
11. **Letting the ideas sort themselves found no hidden categories.** Grouping the 424 featured ideas by meaning, with no categories given, never produced well-separated groups (silhouette about 0.05 at every size from 6 to 20 groups, where 1 is perfect), and the groups changed when the run was repeated. The loose groups that did appear follow industry (homes and contractors, health, schools and parents, marketing and creators), not business model. The software-to-services shift only showed up because we asked about it directly.
12. **The newsletter era writes differently enough to fool the clustering.** A simple model could tell newsletter-era ideas from earlier ones 85% of the time from their meaning alone, and one group was 59% newsletter ideas with no shared topic (rental moms, burial plots, bank loans). Removing each era's average before grouping fixed that without changing how well the groups matched industries.
13. **Four titles were dates.** In June and July 2026, four emails put the date where the title usually goes ("June 9, 2026"), so four ideas were named after days. The clustering surfaced it; the parser now falls back to the subject line, and only those four ideas were classified again.
14. **The best theme only shows up with the full pitch.** Once each era's style was subtracted, the 424 featured ideas split into 17 themes. The most stable one (it came back largely intact in 10 reruns) was ideas whose customer is an AI agent: trust scores, sandboxes and a black-box flight recorder for agents. It grew from 3.5% of featured ideas in era 1 to 17.4% (8 of 46) in the newsletter before Sep 12. Clustering all 1,433 ideas by title alone never found it; a one-line title isn't enough to tell "a tool *for* agents" from "a tool that *uses* agents".
15. **Confident isn't the same as right.** Asked "is this product's customer an AI agent?", Jev was just as decisive from a title alone (96.5% of answers below 0.2 or above 0.8) as with the full pitch (98.6%), yet 8 of 424 answers flipped once it read the pitch. "Owner's manuals for AI agents" sounds like a product for agents but is written for the people who run them; "A chatbot that learns to sell from your own customers" sounds like a sales tool but is an agent that supervises another agent.
