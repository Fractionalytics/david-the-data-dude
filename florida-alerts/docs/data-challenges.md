# Data challenges: florida-alerts

The "inside look" behind the chart: what got in the way, in roughly the order it came up.

## Finding the data

1. **The obvious pages only show today.** FDLE's "Active Alerts" lists the alerts that are open right now, and the per-type pages (AMBER, Silver, Purple, Missing Child) explain each plan without listing past alerts. There is no public history of alerts and their outcomes on the current site.
2. **The Silver Alert "Monthly Reports" page is empty.** It still exists at `/silver-alert-plan/monthly-reports`, but after the heading there's nothing: no table and no PDF links. It looks like the content was lost when the site moved to a new CMS.
3. **The newsletter archive is a broken frame.** MEPIC's Advisory Board newsletter page embeds an iframe pointing at `/mcic/AdvisoryBoardNewsletter.aspx`, which now returns a 404. The only newsletter linked anywhere is Issue 17 (Summer 2026), on the FMCDF site.
4. **"Recovered" isn't "found because of the alert."** Issue 17 gives one statewide figure: in 2025, MEPIC activated 323 state alerts and 321 missing people were recovered. That covers everyone who came home by any route. It doesn't say how many were found *because of* the alert, and that's the part of the question that matters.
5. **The attribution data is a list of wins only.** FDLE's Silver Alert "Success Stories" pages (2014–2022) describe how each person was found: a citizen saw a highway sign, a deputy recognized the plate from the BOLO, and so on. There are 9 to 38 dated entries a year, 197 in all. They count only the successes, with no denominator, and they stop after 2022.
6. **The newsletters were hiding at guessable URLs.** No page links to them, but a web search turned up `/MEPIC/Documents/2021-Fall`. Trying all 32 `<year>-<season>` combinations for 2019–2026 found 13 live issues (Spring 2020 to Fall 2024). Issue 17 (Summer 2026) lives only on FMCDF's site.
7. **The lost monthly reports survive in the Internet Archive, mostly.** The Wayback Machine indexed 120 Silver Alert monthly-report PDFs, 12 a year from 2011 to 2020. Partway through the download, it returned a 404 for October 2012: the index lists the snapshot, but the archive can't serve it. Earlier, the archive's index went "Temporarily Offline" (503) for a while.
8. **FDLE says its own "direct recovery" count is a floor.** Each monthly report flags recoveries that were "directly recovered as a result of the Silver Alert", then adds that agencies don't always tell FDLE, so others "may also have been aided by the alert". Any "the alert found them X% of the time" figure is a lower bound.
9. **"Silver Alert" means two different things.** The monthly reports count only *state* Silver Alerts (a vehicle involved, highway signs activated). Local agencies issue their own local Silver Alerts as well, and some newsletter boxes say "State Silver Alerts" while others just say "Silver Alerts". Whether 287 alerts in 2021 includes local ones has to be checked before comparing years.
10. **The newsletters' numbers come in different shapes.** Each issue has a stat box, but the period varies ("so far in 2021", "in 2021", or no period at all). Some numbers are spelled out ("TWO HUNDRED…"), some are cumulative "since inception" totals, and some issues (Spring 2020, Winter 2022, Summer 2024) have no stat box at all.

## Reading the monthly reports

11. **The running "direct recoveries" total goes backwards, because FDLE changed the definition.** The total reads 45 in June 2011 and 30 in July 2011. That July, the reports start a separate "indirect recovery" count (17), and direct plus indirect (47) carries on from the old 45. The later definitions say a *direct* recovery came from the state alert itself, mainly highway message signs. An *indirect* recovery is the local agency finding the person through police communications or the media. So 2011 can't be compared with later years, and the per-year table starts in 2012. There's also a small dip from 94 to 93 between December 2014 and January 2015, unexplained.
12. **The flag was hiding in plain sight.** At first it looked as if no row was marked "direct", so the yearly counts were built from FDLE's cumulative totals. During spot-check prep, one row's recovery location read "Miami, FL\*": a single asterisk marks a direct recovery and a double asterisk an indirect one, stuck to the end of the place name. That gives two independent counts. They match exactly in 6 of 9 years (2012, 2013, 2017–2020). 2014 is off by 2 (19 marked vs 21), 2015 by 2 (23 vs 21), and 2016 by 5 (25 vs 30). Over 2012–2020 that's 217 marked against 222 from the totals. (The first count was 216. One asterisk sat on the second line of a wrapped place name, "Palm Beach Gardens," / "FL\*", and was lost until the parser learned to join wrapped lines.) The biggest single gap is November 2016: FDLE's running total jumped by 7 (135 to 142), but only 2 of the month's 15 rows carry an asterisk. David checked the original PDF by hand and found no other marks and no note explaining the jump. Recoveries reported to FDLE late, and credited to the month the news arrived, would explain it, but nothing in the report says so.
13. **Totals drift away from the rows.** In 4 of the first 58 reports, the number of case rows doesn't match the report's own "Total for [month]": March 2011 (14 stated, 13 rows), July 2011 (11 vs 10), December 2014 (24 vs 22), and September 2012, which has no monthly total at all. November and December 2018 also have no monthly total. The "since implementation (October 2008)" total looked as if it vanished after June 2012, but it had only been reworded ("since 2008 inception of Florida Silver Alert Plan"), and the parser missed it until we caught it.
14. **The wording changed under the parser twice.** The November 2019 report writes "Direct Recovery - Total to date 227" with no equals sign, followed by "Indirect Recovery - Total to date = 45". The first regex skipped ahead to the next "=" and read 45 as the *direct* total. Every change in a cumulative series now gets checked for jumps.
15. **FDLE seems to have stopped counting indirect recoveries.** The indirect total sits at 45 from May 2017 through December 2020, more than three and a half years without a single new indirect recovery, which is implausible. October 2016 reads 64, between two 44s, almost certainly a typo for 44.
16. **FDLE's own totals disagree slightly.** The yearly totals and the year-over-year change in the since-2008 total match exactly in 7 of 9 years. 2016 is off by 1 (245 vs 246) and 2017 by 2 (273 vs 271).
17. **The trail goes cold in 2021.** FDLE kept publishing monthly reports: a June 2021 snapshot of the 2021 page links reports for January through May 2021. The Internet Archive never saved those PDFs, though, and FDLE's current site returns 404 for every old report URL (even December 2020) and has no 2021 or 2022 folder. From 2021 on, the only Silver Alert numbers are the yearly totals in the newsletters.

## Reading the newsletters

18. **"Direct recovery" got broader over time.** In the Silver monthly reports (2011–2020), police finding someone through law-enforcement communications counted as *indirect*. The Spring 2024 newsletter defines a direct recovery as "when the public has seen an alert and contacted law enforcement, *or* if law enforcement has recovered the missing person due to an issued BOLO". A 2023 rate (51 of 480, 11%) and a 2015 rate don't measure the same thing. The Summer 2023 issue gives a third, narrower version for AMBER: a direct recovery "is only possible when a citizen sees the alert".
19. **The same year, two different counts.** For 2020, Spring 2021's stat box says 33 Missing Child Alerts and Fall 2021's article says 34. The AMBER totals since inception don't add up either: 234 in Fall 2020 and 269 by December 2021 implies 35 new alerts, but the yearly boxes give 19 (2020) plus 10 (2021), which is 29.
20. **No two issues report the same thing.** 2020 and 2021 get full-year boxes covering AMBER, Missing Child and Silver. 2022 has no year-end box, only AMBER and Missing Child counts in later articles. 2023 adds Purple Alerts and gives one all-types direct-recovery figure. 2024 has only a July–September quarter, and 2025 lumps "323 state alerts" together without naming the types. Only 2020, 2021 and 2023 have a usable "alerts vs. direct recoveries" figure.
21. **Typed, then checked by code.** Because of all that, the 44 newsletter figures were transcribed by hand into `newsletter_stats.csv`, each with its page and exact quote. A script confirms every quote appears on that page and every number appears in its quote. It caught a planted error in testing. Six numbers written out in words were checked by eye.

## The national AMBER reports

22. **Five reports sit behind a "no robots" sign.** DOJ's AMBER site links 19 annual reports (2006–2024). The ones for 2019, 2020, 2021, 2023 and 2024 are hosted on missingkids.org, whose robots.txt disallows `/content/dam/missingkids/pdfs/`, so the scraper didn't fetch them. The other 14 came from ojp.gov. David downloaded the five by hand in a browser, and the same pipeline then read all 19.
23. **A broken link on a government page.** The 2017 report's link resolves to amberalert.ojp.gov and returns 403 Forbidden. The same file sits at the same path on ojjdp.ojp.gov, where the 2018 report lives.
24. **Florida's outcomes aren't broken out.** The national reports give recovery outcomes for the whole country only. For Florida, they give just the number of AMBER Alerts it issued (6 to 18 a year). They don't always agree with FDLE, either: NCMEC counts 13 Florida AMBER Alerts in 2022, while FDLE's newsletter says 12.
25. **One summary page is word salad.** The 2012 report's summary page is laid out in columns, and the extracted text interleaves them ("Of the 16 of which As of Ma remained AMBER A…"). The 2012 figures come from its success-stories section instead, and that year's recovery count is left blank.
26. **The search engine's summary got a number wrong.** A web search summary said the 2016 report covered "170" AMBER Alerts. The report itself says 179. Every number in the dataset comes from the source PDFs, not from search summaries.
27. **Two official counts of Florida AMBER Alerts never agree.** Where both sources cover the same year, they're off by one or two every time:

    | Year | FDLE newsletter | NCMEC national report |
    |---|---|---|
    | 2020 | 19 | 18 |
    | 2021 | 10 | 8 |
    | 2022 | 12 | 13 |
    | 2023 | 8 | 7 |

    NCMEC counts "originating activations" by the state where the alert was first issued. FDLE counts the alerts it issued. Neither source explains the gap. It might come from regional or local alerts, multi-state alerts, or year-boundary timing, but that's a guess. It's small, but it means "how many AMBER Alerts did Florida issue?" has two official answers.
28. **The newest reports moved the state table.** In 2023 and 2024, the first "Number of Alerts" on the page is a chart heading ("Number of Alerts and Children"), and the real state table is a page later. The extraction got the right numbers (Florida 7 and 9) only because it fell through to the next page, so those two were checked against the PDF by eye.

## Chasing the years after 2020

29. **Every route past 2020 dead-ends, except FDLE's birthday announcements.**
    - The monthly reports are gone: FDLE returns 404, and the Archive never saved the 2021 PDFs.
    - The success-story pages stop at 2022: the 2023–2025 pages return 404.
    - The Archive's copy of FDLE's newsletter folder holds the same 13 issues we already had.
    - FDLE posted a press release every October for the program's anniversary, with running totals. Those continue through **October 2023**. FDLE's October and November news pages for 2024 and 2025 carry no Silver Alert release.

    So the latest direct-recovery figure is from October 2023, and it's rounded: "more than 3,200" alerts and "more than 300" direct recoveries since 2008.
30. **The running rate barely moves in 15 years.** Lining up every running total FDLE published gives 8.4% (2012), 9.7% (2015), 10.4% (2020), 9.8% (2021), 9.5% (2022) and about 9.4% (2023). The October 2015 release (1,134 alerts, 110 direct) matches FDLE's September 2015 monthly report exactly, so the press releases and the monthly reports share the same ledger.
31. **"Out of state" depends on how you read a place name.** The first pass only recognized a two-letter state code after a comma ("Valdosta, GA") and found 149 people recovered outside Florida. Recognizing full state names too ("Georgia", "Morris, Illinois") raised it to 168, across 22 states, as far as Arizona, Minnesota and Washington. One "Washington" could be the state or D.C. Six rows had the *next row's number* in the recovery column, a sign that a field was missing and everything shifted left. Eleven said "Active", "Person Remains Missing" or "Unknown". Still unverified (spot checks 4 and 5).
32. **One table, seven ways to break it.** Fixing the case-row parser meant handling every way FDLE's table varies over ten years. Each fix was checked by diffing old output against new:
    - Row numbers without a period ("8" instead of "8."). This dropped December 2014's rows 8 and 10. The fixes recovered 4 dropped alerts in all, the other two in March and July 2011.
    - Age and sex written backwards ("Male/ 88"). This blanked 3 ages in July 2018.
    - Place names wrapped onto two lines ("Chadds Ford Township," / "PA").
    - Agency names wrapped onto two lines ("Naples Police and Emergency" / "Services Department"), which pushed the recovery place into a spare column in 6 rows.
    - The agency and last-seen town merged into one line ("Jacksonville Beach PD Jacksonville Beach, FL").
    - Empty numbered rows at the bottom of a table ("12. 13. 14. …").
    - A stray comma after a complete place ("Bal Harbour FL,").

    After the fixes: 2,219 alerts, and every report with a stated monthly total matches it except October 2018, where row 11 is missing from the extracted text (21 of 22). Four rows have an empty recovery cell (spot check 19). The out-of-state total held at 168 across 22 states. The lone "Washington" turned out to be Puyallup, WA, about 2,500 miles in a straight line from Spring Hill, Florida, where the man went missing. That recovery is marked as found because of the alert.
33. **The archive pushed back on the human checker, too.** The pipeline downloaded all 119 reports from the Wayback Machine one at a time, a few seconds apart. Then David's hand spot checks, clicking through the same archived PDFs in a browser, ran into "429 Too Many Requests", and three checks of far-away recoveries (Minnesota, Washington, and two blank rows) were left unfinished. The only copies of FDLE's own records sit behind someone else's rate limit.
34. **Two charts that seemed to disagree.** In FDLE's success stories, citizens spotted more missing people than police did (107 to 84). But counting how people knew to look, the police BOLO came out on top (54), ahead of highway signs (49). Cross-tabulating the two showed why. When police find someone, the story almost always names the BOLO (52 of 84). When a citizen finds someone, more than half the stories just say they "recognized him from the Silver Alert", without saying where they saw it (53 of 107). Those unnamed stories drop out of a channel count, so the police channel looked bigger than it was. Counted together, the public channels (signs 49 + lottery terminals 14 = 63) beat the BOLO's 54, and that's how card 7 now shows it.
