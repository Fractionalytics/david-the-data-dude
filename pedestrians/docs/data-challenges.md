# Data challenges: an inside look

The data problems that surprised us, logged as they happened.

1. **The SUV boom came twice.** The story says sedans fell and SUVs rose. But in the government's sales data, pickups, SUVs and minivans already outsold cars in every year from 2001 to 2007, and pedestrian deaths stayed flat then (4,675–4,901 a year). The sedan collapse people remember (cars from 7.7M in 2014 to 2.7M in 2025) is a second wave, made of crossovers. Any chart has to show both waves, or the timing looks better than it is.
2. **Whose count of the dead?** IIHS and NHTSA both build their pedestrian counts from the same federal crash census, but they don't always match: 7,367 deaths in 2023 by IIHS's count, 7,314 in NHTSA's 2023 report. The crash files are revised after their first release, so the year you download matters.
3. **No one counts phones in crashes.** Smartphone ownership is easy to chart (35% of adults in 2011, 91% by 2024), but there's no reliable count of crashes where a phone was involved. The phone link can only be shown as timing.
4. **The world's road data is behind a membership.** The international database every country comparison uses (the ITF's IRTAD) offers only a selection free; the full annual series by country needs a membership. So the comparison rests on two years, 2013 and 2022, as published in a CDC study, rather than a line for every year.
5. **Europe got SUVs too.** The obvious explanation for America's rise in pedestrian deaths is SUVs. But SUVs went from 12% to 56% of new cars in Europe between 2010 and 2024, and European hoods got taller, while European pedestrian deaths fell. Whatever the US did differently, it wasn't simply buying SUVs.
6. **Exposure: the denominator nobody has.** A fair comparison would count deaths per mile walked, but no country measures walking well. Miles driven is the next best thing: US vehicle miles per person rose just 1.4% from 2013 to 2022, while pedestrian deaths per person rose 50%. For other countries the only comparable driving figures we found are from FHWA tables nearly 20 years old.
7. **No yearly ruler for hoods.** Europe has an average new-car hood height for every year (76.9 cm in 2010, 83.8 cm in 2024). The US has nothing like it in public: the best is a Consumer Reports analysis of industry data saying truck hoods rose at least 11% since 2000.
8. **The $3 isn't a price tag.** Advocates call the US pedestrian rule a "$3 fix". In NHTSA's own analysis, $3 isn't what a safer hood costs: its teardown found no visible difference in design or assembly, only a little extra weight, so the $2.86–$4.08 is the lifetime fuel cost of that weight.
9. **Doors that close on robots.** The Federal Register's website sends automated readers to a block page, the ITF's report returned 403, and the European DaCoTA project's site no longer exists. We used the government printing office's copy of the proposal (govinfo.gov), the Federal Register's own search API, and secondary summaries for the EU's early-2000s figures, and didn't work around any block.
10. **Two definitions of "truck".** EPA says 66% of 2024 model-year vehicles were trucks; the Commerce Department's sales data says 81% of 2024 sales. Both are right: EPA counts many small SUVs as cars, and one counts production by model year while the other counts sales by calendar year.
11. **The road map was redrawn in 2015.** The federal crash files describe each road by its "function" (interstate, arterial, collector, local), but the variable was replaced in 2015 with a new one using FHWA's newer road classes, and the old one had no code for rural freeways that aren't interstates. We built one mapping across both. Even so, the shift shows: deaths on local streets fell from 938 in 2015 to 633 in 2019 while arterial deaths rose from 3,121 to 4,000, partly because states were re-classifying their roads at the same time. Arterial's rise from 49% of deaths in 2000 to 62% in 2024 is real in direction, but a few points of it may be paperwork.
12. **"No sidewalk" really means "none noted".** The sidewalk field only starts in 2014, and its answers are "yes", "none noted" and "unknown". In 2024, 4,402 of 7,080 pedestrian deaths (62%) had no sidewalk noted, but that mixes roads with no sidewalk and reports that didn't record one.
13. **More drivers who were never found.** The striking vehicle's body type was unknown for about 6–9% of pedestrian deaths through 2019, then 12.4% in 2020 (811 deaths), and still 10.5% in 2024. Most are likely hit-and-runs, where no vehicle was ever inspected. Light-truck shares have to be quoted as a share of the deaths where the vehicle type is known.
14. **The newest year isn't settled.** The 2024 file NHTSA posts counts 7,080 pedestrian deaths. Crash files are revised after their first release (see 2), so 2024 may still move. Every earlier year we checked (2000, 2005, 2009, 2015, 2019, 2022, 2023) matched IIHS's published counts to within one death.
15. **Europe's total that isn't a total.** Eurostat publishes an "EU-27" pedestrian death count back to 1999, but it's just the sum of whichever countries reported that year. Lithuania is missing until 2012, Slovakia from 2011 to 2015, Malta from 2011 to 2014, and nine countries are missing in 2000. Only from 2016 are all 27 in. So we divide reporting countries' deaths by reporting countries' population, and note who's missing. The gaps are mostly high-death Eastern countries, so the early EU rate is, if anything, too low.
16. **Europe used to be worse.** In 2000 the EU's pedestrian death rate (2.29 per 100,000, even without nine Eastern members) was higher than America's (1.69). The lines crossed around 2010 (1.34 vs. 1.39). America wasn't always the outlier: Europe fell by two-thirds while America went up.
17. **Brexit broke the UK line.** Eurostat's UK series stops at 2018. From 2019 we use Britain's own Department for Transport counts, which cover Great Britain only, so Northern Ireland's roughly 16 pedestrian deaths a year are missing and the UK rate after 2018 is about 3% low.
18. **What counts as an SUV depends on who's counting.** For 2024, ICCT puts SUVs at 48% of new EU cars, JATO at 54% of Europe's, and T&E at 55.5% of the EU, UK and Norway's. For 2010 it's 9% (ICCT) against 11.5% (T&E). Each keeps its own list of which models are SUVs, and crossovers are where they disagree. We chart ICCT's because it's the only one with every year back to 2001, and call it the conservative count.
19. **Charts without tables.** The only yearly SUV series for the EU (ICCT) and the only yearly hood-height series (T&E) are published as charts with no numbers. We recovered ICCT's values from the drawing instructions inside its PDF, and read T&E's off the chart's pixels, to within about 0.1 cm. T&E's report says "click here for year-by-year data", but the link points back to the same page.
20. **An SUV in Europe is a car; in America it's a truck.** Europe files SUVs under "passenger cars", so its SUV share is a slice of cars. America's sales data puts SUVs, crossovers, pickups and minivans together as "light trucks" (81% of 2024 sales). So the two shares measure different things: the US one includes pickups and minivans, and Europe registers most pickups as vans, outside "cars" entirely.
21. **What counts as a pedestrian?** America's federal crash data puts people on wheelchairs, skateboards and scooters in their own category; the international databases count them as pedestrians. That's why the CDC's US rate for 2022 (2.33 per 100,000, 7,768 deaths) ran above ours (2.27): it adds about 250 "personal conveyance" deaths to a preliminary pedestrian count. With them added, our 2013 rate is exactly the CDC's 1.55. The US-vs-Europe charts now use the international definition.
22. **A category that vanished for two years.** In 2020 and 2021 only, the federal files split "person on a personal conveyance" into three codes (motorized, non-motorized, unknown), so a plain count of the old code showed zero such deaths in those years. Counting all three restores 182 and 214.
23. **Keeping the club the same.** Eurostat's "EU-27" pedestrian total in 2000 adds up only the 18 members that reported. Comparing 2000 with 2024 that way mixes a smaller club with a bigger one. We rebuilt the EU line from the 18 members that reported in every year from 2000 to 2024, and split it into Western members (before 2004) and Eastern ones. The headline holds: the EU fell from 2.29 to 0.75 per 100,000, and the US rose from 1.72 to 2.16.
24. **A drunk test that changed its decimal point.** The crash files record each pedestrian's blood alcohol, but in 2015 the unit switched from hundredths to thousandths. A reading of 80 meant 0.80 (a fatal level) before 2015 and the legal limit, 0.080, after. Testing also got thinner: 71% of pedestrians killed in 2009 had a test result, 57% in 2022, and in the newest file (2024) a third of results are simply "not reported". So we used NHTSA's own imputed alcohol files, which fill the gaps statistically, and checked that they match every real test result (they do, once you know NHTSA rounds 0.087 down to 0.08).
25. **Where the pedestrian stood, renumbered.** The "location" field was recoded in 2010, so the old and new codes don't line up one to one. Only three broad groups survive the break cleanly: at an intersection, mid-block on the road, and off the travel lanes. Even after 2010, "mid-block, crosswalk availability unknown" fell from 469 deaths to 197 while "mid-block, not in a crosswalk" rose, which looks like officers filling the field in more completely, not a change on the street.
26. **The speed limit moved from the crash to the car.** Through 2009 the files give one speed limit per crash; from 2010 they give the limit on the road each vehicle was on. We use the crash's limit through 2009 and the striking vehicle's after, and the share of deaths on roads posted 40 mph or more moves smoothly across the switch (61.8% in 2009, 62.6% in 2010).
27. **More drivers who left, more cars nobody saw.** Hit-and-runs rose from 16.6% of pedestrian deaths in 2009 to 24.4% in 2022, and they account for a third of the whole increase. That also means a growing share of deaths with no vehicle to describe: the striking vehicle's model year is unknown for 1,005 deaths in 2022, against 399 in 2009. Before 2009 the hit-and-run field had several "yes" codes we couldn't check against labels, so the series starts in 2009.
28. **The distraction field went quiet.** From 2010 the crash files say whether each driver was distracted, and by what. But for the drivers who hit and killed pedestrians, the answer "not reported" or "unknown" went from 19% of deaths in 2010-12 to 62% in 2020-22 ("not reported" alone went from 1,106 to 10,572 over the three years). The jump starts in 2016-17, which looks like states changing what their crash forms record, not drivers changing. Among drivers whose status is known, phone distraction rose from 1.2% to 2.0%, but that's only 42 phone-distracted drivers a year in 2010-12 and 54 in 2020-22, out of about 4,500 and 7,200 deaths a year. With most answers missing, the field can't say much about phones either way.
29. **The homelessness count is behind a bot wall.** HUD publishes its state-by-state homelessness counts as a spreadsheet, but its website answered every automated request with an Amazon "challenge" page instead of the file. We didn't try to get around it, so the state homelessness test waits for someone to download the file by hand.
30. **The Census door now wants a key.** To test whether deaths grew faster in poorer or car-free neighborhoods, we needed each census tract's poverty and car ownership from the Census Bureau's data API. That API used to answer small requests without signing up; this time it returned a "Missing Key" page instead of data, and even its robots.txt came back "Request Rejected". The tract maps themselves downloaded fine, and 98.7–99.7% of pedestrian deaths in 2009–11 and 2020–22 carry coordinates that land in a 2010 tract (25–54 deaths a year have none). The neighborhood tests wait on a free API key.
31. **A hurricane in the baseline.** To test whether homelessness explains where pedestrian deaths rose, we compared each state's unsheltered homeless count in 2009 with 2022. But 2009 wasn't a normal year everywhere: Louisiana's count was still swollen four years after Hurricane Katrina, and Florida's by the recession, so both show big "drops" in unsheltered homelessness (−160 and −128 per 100,000) that mostly reflect an unusual starting point. The workbook also changed its column names: older editions label them "Unsheltered Homeless, 2009", this one just "Unsheltered Homeless" on a sheet per year, which broke our first reading of it.
32. **A third of America has no bus riders, on paper.** To test whether transit riders' neighborhoods saw bigger rises, we split census tracts into five equal-population groups by the share of workers who commute by public transportation. But 29% of Americans live in a tract where the Census survey found no transit commuters at all, so the whole bottom fifth, and part of the second, is tracts tied at exactly zero. Which zero-tracts land in the bottom fifth then depends on how ties are broken; our rule breaks them by tract number, which puts low-numbered states first. Breaking the ties at random instead gave the same verdict. The night-shift measure has the opposite problem: it is a small share everywhere (the top fifth starts at 6.7% of workers leaving home between midnight and 5am, the bottom fifth tops out at 1.5%), so in a small tract it rests on a handful of survey answers.
33. **Two official overdose tables, and neither covers both decades.** CDC's downloadable state table of age-adjusted overdose death rates stops in 2016; its newer state map covers 1999, 2005 and 2014–2024, skipping 2009–2011. So the overdose test stitches the two together (2009–11 from one, 2020–22 from the other). Where they overlap, 2015 and 2016 agree to within 0.05 per 100,000, but the map's 2014 figures for Arkansas and Arizona look swapped (18.2 and 12.6, against 12.6 and 18.2 in the table), and some small states show a rate of 0 in 1999 and 2005 where the number was withheld. None of those years enter the test.
34. **The drug codes changed four years after the "overhaul".** The crash files' drug-test results were reorganized in 2018: instead of at most three results per person, a separate file lists every result (up to 33 for one pedestrian in 2024) and which sample was tested. But the drug codes themselves stayed the same until 2022, when they switched to a new numbering (fentanyl went from 151 to 1059) with hundreds of newly named substances, including caffeine and nicotine filed under "stimulants". Counting the whole stimulant class would have made 2022 jump for a reason that has nothing to do with meth or cocaine, so we matched the named drugs across both schemes by hand. Testing also thinned: 60% of pedestrians killed in 2009–11 had a valid drug result, 56% in 2020–22 and 49% in 2024.
35. **No meth count by state before 2015, and most states missing in 2015.** We wanted each state's methamphetamine overdose rate for 2009–11. Outside CDC WONDER (which needs terms accepted by hand), CDC publishes psychostimulant deaths for that period only for the nation as a whole. Its state-by-drug counts start in 2015, so the test fell back to 2015 vs. 2022. And in 2015, CDC withheld the meth count for 27 states because too few of their death certificates named the drug, including California, Texas, Florida and Arizona, where meth matters most. Three more (DC, New Hampshire, Rhode Island) had fewer than 10 deaths and were withheld. Louisiana has no drug-specific rows at all in any year. That left 21 states, and CDC's own notes say state comparisons of drug-specific rates "should not be made".
36. **New York is two places in the overdose file.** CDC's provisional overdose table lists "New York" without New York City, and New York City as its own row. Read naively, New York's 2015 overdose total would be 1,802 instead of 2,761. We add the two back together. Vermont, meanwhile, shows exactly 0 meth deaths in 2015 with no suppression note, where other small counts are hidden.
37. **The "old cars" number was the whole fleet aging.** Deaths involving vehicles 15 or more years old went from 471 to 1,863 a year, which looked like a lead. But the share of old vehicles roughly doubled in *every* fatal crash, not just pedestrian ones: 16.9% → 34.3% of vehicles in crashes with no pedestrian, vs. 14.2% → 27.5% among vehicles that hit one. After the 2008 recession Americans kept cars longer (mean age in those crashes 9.1 → 11.2 years). Only a control group caught it.
38. **Fleeing drivers hide their license status.** A driver who hits a pedestrian and drives away has no known license status, and hit-and-run grew from 17.5% to 23.7% of pedestrian deaths. So license status is unknown for 16% of pedestrian deaths in 2020–22, against 2% of other fatal crashes. The not-licensed share we can measure (8.7% → 12.7%) rose no faster than in other crashes, but the drivers most likely to be unlicensed are exactly the ones we can't see.
39. **The last census on old tract lines is 2015–19.** Measuring tract population growth needs the same tract boundaries at both ends, and census tracts were redrawn for 2020. The last five-year survey on 2010 boundaries is 2015–19, so the "after" population is about three years older than the 2020–22 deaths it's divided into. Twenty-five tract codes in 2015–19 don't appear in 2008–12 (and 26 the other way), corrections the Census Bureau made between releases; they're left out.
40. **"Outside any city" shrank, and it isn't because people moved.** FARS gives each crash a city code, with 0 meaning outside a city. The share of fatal crashes coded 0 fell from 55% in 2009 to 51% in 2015 and 47% in 2022, while the number of distinct city codes rose from 2,079 to 2,231. Cities annex land and states change how they code, so a city's death count can grow just because its borders did. For the city tests we place crashes inside fixed 2010 city boundaries using the crash coordinates instead.
41. **Whether a dead pedestrian gets a drug test depends on the state.** In FARS, the share of pedestrians killed with a drug result ranges from about 3% in North Carolina to 96% in Nevada (per the meth evidence review). A national "share positive" mixes states that test almost everyone with states that test almost no one, so it can move just because the mix changes. Only four states tested at least 70% every year from 2009 to 2022 (Maryland, New Jersey, Washington, West Virginia). In those four, the stimulant share still rose, from about 8% to about 21%.
42. **Two official numbers for Germany's pedestrian deaths.** Eurostat lists 375 pedestrians killed in Germany in 2022; reports based on Germany's own statistics office give about 366–368. Same country, same year, two government sources, about 2% apart. The trend we use (591 in 2009 to under 400) doesn't change, but it's a reminder that "the official number" depends on which office you ask.
43. **"After dark" can mean two things.** Measured by the clock (6pm to 6am), 84% of the rise in US pedestrian deaths from 2009 to 2022 happened after dark. Measured by what the police report says about the light (dark, daylight, dawn, dusk), it's 87%. In summer, 6 to 9pm is still light, so the clock version undercounts. And "daylight deaths stayed flat" is true from 2000 (1,671 a year) to 2024 (1,653), but not from 2009, a low year (1,241), after which they rose 35%.
44. **The drivers who hit pedestrians are the least-tested people in the file.** Viewers asked whether the meth was in the drivers. Only 14% of drivers who killed a pedestrian in 2020–22 have a drug result, against 38% of drivers in other fatal crashes and 56% of the pedestrians themselves. Most drivers who hit a pedestrian survive, and survivors are tested far less than the dead; about 85 pedestrian deaths a year have no driver record at all, mostly hit-and-runs. Among those tested, stimulant-positive drivers rose less (4.4% → 9.9%) than in other fatal crashes (6.9% → 16.7%), but with so few tests that's weak evidence either way.
45. **Fewer tests in 2024 made the stimulant share rise.** Among pedestrians killed, the share with a drug result fell from 55% in 2022 to 49% in 2024, and the stimulant share among those tested rose from 28.5% to 31.4%. Counted against every pedestrian killed instead, stimulant-positive deaths stayed about flat (15.7% → 15.4%). Which denominator you choose changes the direction, but neither shows the fall the meth explanation would predict.
46. **Amphetamine has two sources.** "Stimulant" in the drug tests includes amphetamine, which is both a prescription drug (Adderall) and what meth breaks down into. Splitting them out: methamphetamine-positive pedestrians rose 17.1 points (2.5% → 19.6% of those tested), amphetamine without meth only 0.8 points. Meth is 89% of the rise.
47. **Two official "stimulant death" rates for the US, five times apart.** CDC counts about 10 deaths per 100,000 Americans a year that involve a psychostimulant like meth (2021–22). WHO's estimate for deaths from "amphetamine use disorders" is about 2 per 100,000 (2021). Both are right for what they measure: CDC counts any death certificate that mentions the drug, and WHO counts deaths where the stimulant is the underlying cause, while most US stimulant deaths also involve fentanyl. WHO's version is built to compare countries, and on it the US was 9 times the EU rate in 2009 and 23 times by 2021. Europe's drug agency, which publishes the EU's own figures, blocks automated readers and even showed a "verify you are human" check in a normal browser, and the comparable IHME series is marked not for redistribution.
48. **A test that missed its own gate by 0.0006.** For the speed-camera test we said in advance: if camera cities and their comparison cities were already on different paths before 2009–11 by more than 0.3 deaths per 100,000, the test can't be scored. The difference came out at 0.3006. The other numbers looked like a pass (camera cities +15% against +75%), but the rule was written so a borderline result can't be talked into a verdict, and it wasn't. Part of the reason is that Washington DC has run speed cameras since 2001, so its "before" years were already camera years.
49. **Nothing in a camera-ban state looks like New York.** Matching compares each camera city with similar cities where state law bans cameras. But those states (Texas, New Jersey, Wisconsin, Nevada) have no city near New York's density (about 10,400 people per km²). The closest is Newark, at 4,400. Dense cities rose least whether or not they had cameras (San Francisco −11%, Pittsburgh +9%), so the data can't separate "cameras" from "the kind of city that adopts cameras."
50. **"Similar use" is true for stimulants, not for opioids.** Last-year use of meth and amphetamines (0.8% vs. 0.8%) and cocaine (1.5% vs. 1.6%) is about the same in the US and EU. But Europe estimates 860,000 high-risk opioid users (about 1,900 per million people), while US estimates of opioid use disorder run 6.7–7.6 million (about 20,000–23,000 per million). The measures differ, but most of the 15× opioid death gap is the size of the dependent population, not deaths per user.
51. **The survey year moved the numbers.** Earlier notes used NSDUH 2022 (meth 1.0%, cocaine 1.9% last-year use). NSDUH 2024 has 0.8% and 1.5%. The "about the same as Europe" conclusion survives; the exact figures don't.
52. **Europe's report gives two heroin-death counts for one year.** The European Drug Report 2025 puts EU heroin-involved deaths in 2023 at about 1,200 in one table (18 countries) and about 1,600 ("provisional") in another. Any heroin ratio is a range, about 2–3× for the US.
53. **WHO and CDC split the same death differently.** WHO gives the US a cocaine death rate of 3.2 per 100,000; CDC counts 7.4 cocaine-involved deaths per 100,000. WHO assigns each death to one cause, so a cocaine-plus-fentanyl death counts as an opioid death; CDC counts it under both.
54. **Better death certificates look like more deaths.** Before 2014, US death certificates named the specific drug in only 75–79% of overdose deaths; by 2022 it was 96%. Part of the rise in deaths "involving meth" or "involving fentanyl" is better reporting, not more deaths.
55. **The US treats more opioid users per person, not fewer.** As a share of those who need it, treatment coverage looks far worse in the US (25% vs. 59% in the EU). Per person, the US has about 6,900 people on methadone or buprenorphine per million against about 1,140 in the EU, roughly 6× more. The low coverage share comes from the much larger number who need treatment.
56. **Three sources disagree on whether Germany out-prescribes the US.** On the UN narcotics board's daily-dose measure, Germany is at 83% of the US (2020–22). A pharmacy-sales study (Ju et al. 2022) has Germany passing the US in 2019 (879 vs. 738 morphine milligrams per 1,000 people per day). A third study adds raw milligrams across opioids of different strength and can't compare countries at all. The likely cause of the split is how each converts fentanyl patches.
57. **Fentanyl in meth is rare; fentanyl with meth is common.** Fentanyl turned up in under 1% of crystal meth samples and none of the crack samples in one US drug-checking study (718 samples), yet 68.8% of US meth deaths also involve an opioid. That points to people using both drugs, not to meth laced with fentanyl.
58. **The US counts more kinds of drug death.** The US "overdose death" count includes any drug, prescription medicines included; the EU's "drug-induced deaths" count mainly illegal drugs. So the US-vs-EU ratio for deaths without opioids (about 14×) is overstated by an amount nobody has measured.
59. **Key sources were blocked.** The DEA's threat assessment, EUDA's wastewater page, a BMJ review, Pew's methadone comparison and RAND's fentanyl report all returned "403 Forbidden" to our research bot. Their figures are either missing or marked unverified.
60. **Europe's meth country barely loses anyone to meth.** Czechia has about 37,200 high-risk meth users, more than 90% of whom inject, yet its meth deaths stayed at 10–17 a year from 2013 to 2021 (33 in 2022). That's about 0.9 deaths per 1,000 heavy users a year, against 19.5 in the US (6.0 without opioids). Same drug, same counting logic, about 20× the death rate per heavy user.
61. **America doesn't have many more heavy meth users per person.** The US has 1.78 million people with meth use disorder (5,300 per million, NSDUH 2023); Czechia has about 3,500 high-risk meth users per million. A 1.5× difference can't explain a 33× death gap.
62. **The US survey changed how it measures addiction.** NSDUH counted 741,000 people with meth use disorder in 2015 (ages 18–64, old criteria) and 1,784,000 in 2023 (ages 12+, new criteria). The two numbers can't be compared, so "how many heavy users" over time is out of reach.
63. **Two "official" Czech heavy-user counts.** The European Drug Report says Czechia has 37,900 high-risk meth users; the Czech national report says 37,200. We used the Czech figure.
64. **WHO's count runs far below national counts.** WHO's single-cause estimate of meth-type deaths is 8× lower than Australia's own count of deaths involving meth, and 4.7× lower than CDC's for the US. Comparisons have to use one method across all countries, or each country's own method, never a mix.
65. **One country is two-thirds of Europe's cocaine deaths.** Spain supplied 621 of the 956 cocaine deaths counted across 20 European countries in 2022 (65%), with 11% of the EU's population. Its forensic register finds more deaths, not necessarily more cocaine harm, so "Europe's cocaine death rate" mostly measures how well other countries look.
66. **Spain's register grew into its numbers.** It covered 20% of the country in 1998 and 100% by 2022, so a rise in Spanish cocaine deaths over time partly reflects wider coverage.
67. **England counts cocaine deaths better and already has more of them.** England & Wales had 19.9 cocaine deaths per million in 2024, above the US rate for cocaine deaths with no opioid involved (about 13.5, an estimate). But 21.4% of England's drug-poisoning death registrations name no drug at all, and it publishes no split with and without opioids.
68. **Better death certificates inflate the early meth rise.** In 2009 about 75% of US overdose death certificates named a specific drug, against 96% by 2022. US meth deaths without opioids rose ×3.4 from 2009 to 2015 on paper, before fentanyl was common, but part of that rise is better reporting.
69. **A typo in a CDC table.** A footnote in NCHS Data Brief 474 labels a cocaine table with the code for psychostimulants (T43.6 instead of T40.5). The numbers match cocaine; the label doesn't.
70. **Our estimate of US cocaine deaths without opioids was 32% too low.** With no published split for 2022–24, we applied a 2021–24 average (79% of cocaine deaths also involved opioids) to the 2024 total, and got about 4,600 deaths without opioids. David's CDC WONDER export gave the real number: 6,698, because deaths with opioids fell 34% in 2024 while deaths without them kept rising (5,236 in 2021 to 6,698 in 2024). An average over a period of fast change hides the change. It moved the "unexplained" part of the US–EU cocaine gap from 2.9–11.9× to 4.2–17.4×, and England & Wales from "above" the US no-opioid rate to "about equal" (entry 67).
71. **One click picked eleven drug codes.** The meth export from CDC WONDER came back with 40,907 deaths for 2023, against the 34,855 we expected. Its footer showed why: the whole "T43" group was selected (antidepressants, antipsychotics and other psychotropic drugs), not T43.6 alone. The pre-agreed check caught it before any number was used. The cocaine exports passed: 29,449 for 2023, and the 2018–21 rows match CDC's published Data Brief tables to the death.
72. **A saved web page isn't the report.** The DEA's 2025 National Drug Threat Assessment came through as a two-page printout of its landing page; the report itself is a separate PDF linked from that page.
73. **Nobody publishes when P2P meth took over.** The DEA has profiled seized meth since the 2000s, but its reports are quarterly snapshots scattered across dea.gov (which blocks our bot), state law-enforcement sites and journal articles. We pieced the share of samples made from P2P together from five documents: about 1% (2007, a news report, unverified), 69% (late 2010), 91% (2013), 99.2% (2019) and 99.6% (2022). The years in between are missing, and the samples come from police seizures, which the DEA itself says don't represent all meth.
74. **The "cheaper" part of "cheaper, purer, more plentiful" has no clean number.** We found no national US meth price series. Scattered reports disagree, one even showing San Diego prices rising after 2009, so the claim that P2P made meth cheaper rests on the DEA's words ("low cost"), not on data.
75. **Spain counts drugs, the US counts people.** Spain's forensic reports give, for pedestrians killed, the number positive for each drug (cocaine 19, amphetamine-type 4 in 2023), but one person can be in several groups, so "positive for a stimulant" can only be pinned to a range (19 to 23 of 200, 9.5–11.5%). FARS lets us count each pedestrian once. Both countries test a little over half of pedestrians killed, which makes the comparison fairer than most, but in neither is the untested half known to look like the tested half.
76. **"Most of the gap" depends on which gap.** Since 2000, Europe's pedestrian death rate fell far more (−1.54 per 100,000) than the US rate rose (+0.45), so Europe's improvement moved the lines more. But of the 2024 gap itself (US 2.16 vs. EU 0.75), 59–66% is US pedestrians breaking away from other US road users. Our own US-vs-EU notes had said the first and implied the second; both are now stated separately.
77. **Spain's early toxicology reports came from fewer labs.** The 2010–2013 reports cover cases analysed by the national institute's own labs; from about 2020 the reports add regional forensic institutes. Spain's stimulant-positive share of dead pedestrians rose from 4.0% to 10.5%, but part of that could be who got tested and how sensitive the tests were, not only more drug use.
78. **The "60/40" split moves depending on how you cut it.** Splitting the 2024 US-vs-EU pedestrian gap into "US pedestrians breaking away" vs. "Europe safer for everyone" gives 59% with a 2009 base year, 66% with a 2000 base, anywhere from 51% to 75% depending on the year (2010–2024), and 57% (24–57% across years) if you split the ratio instead of the difference. And if you ask about the *level* in 2024 rather than the change since 2009, most of the gap is "US roads are deadlier for everyone" (2.6× per person for non-pedestrians). We now say "about half to 60%" and name the method (`scripts/gap_split_over_time.py`).
79. **The US survey finds fewer meth users than RAND finds heavy users.** In 2015, the national household survey (NSDUH) counted 1.61 million Americans who used meth at all in the past year. RAND, working from arrestees' urine tests, estimated 3.4 million who used it on at least 4 days in the past month. Deaths per heavy user swing from 4.5 to 1.0 per 1,000 depending on which count you divide by, and Czechia's 0.89 sits at the low end. Which denominator you trust decides most of the US-vs-Czech "mystery".
80. **WONDER hides small counts, and that can flip a state's label.** In the 2024 state data, counts of 1–9 show as "Suppressed". Our first state ranking treated a suppressed cocaine count as larger than a real meth count and mislabeled Hawaii, Montana, Nebraska, Wyoming and North Dakota as cocaine-paired. They are meth-paired (31 meth states, not 26).
81. **A WONDER export can silently drop its drug filter.** The 2018–2024 state-by-year cocaine export came back byte-for-byte identical to the all-overdose export, apart from its timestamp: the cocaine code (T40.5) never made it into the query. Nothing errors; the only signs are the missing "MCD" line in the file's footer and totals that are far too large. We now check every export's footer for its codes and compare national sums with published totals before using it.
82. **"Amphetamines" isn't "meth".** Europe's drug agency reports one figure for amphetamine and meth together (0.8% of adults used one in the past year), and in most of Europe that's amphetamine ("speed"). We compared it with US meth use (also 0.8%) and wrote "meth use is the same" in several notes. It isn't. Meth is rare in Europe outside Czechia and Slovakia, and even Czechia's meth use is 0.5% of ages 15–64 (2022), against 0.8% in the US. What *is* about the same is stimulant use overall: cocaine 1.5% vs. 1.6%, and amphetamine-type 0.8% vs. 0.8%. Europeans use as many stimulants, just different ones. David caught it.
83. **"Fentanyl" in FARS is a family, and some members are hospital drugs.** To count fentanyl among pedestrians killed we matched every FARS drug label containing "fentanyl", "fentanil" or "ANPP" (a fentanyl precursor). That's 13 codes in the old 3-digit scheme and 19 in the new 4-digit one, including analogs like carfentanil and sufentanil, an anesthesia drug. Fentanyl is also a routine trauma painkiller, so a positive test can come from the ambulance. Restricting to pedestrians who died within the first hour barely changed the result (19.7% → 18.4% of stimulant-positive pedestrians), so medical fentanyl isn't driving it.
84. **Putting the US on Europe's death definition.** Europe counts drug deaths more narrowly: poisonings only when a narcotic or stimulant is named, plus deaths coded as drug-use disorders. The US counts poisonings by any drug. Re-counting 2024 US deaths Europe's way, the poisoning part alone is 71,347 (vs. 79,384), which is already 12.6× Europe's rate; the drug-use-disorder deaths can only add to that. One of the three WONDER queries picked the whole F10–F19 block, which includes alcohol, so the first answer was a range: 12.6× to 15.9×. David re-ran it with only the drug codes (F11, F12, F14–F16, F19: 3,178 deaths). On Europe's definition the US had 74,525 drug deaths in 2024, 94% of its own count, or **13.1×** Europe's rate against 14× on the US definition. Nearly all the difference falls in the "everything else" slice (pills and benzodiazepines without a narcotic or stimulant); the deadly pair, fentanyl alone and stimulants without fentanyl are all counted the same way under either definition.
85. **Our "Europe definition" was still a bit wider than Europe's.** A spot check against the protocol's own code table (EMCDDA codification report, PDF p. 8, Table 1) found that the F codes in entry 84 match exactly, but the poisoning pull took every US overdose code (X40–X44, X60–X64, X85, Y10–Y14) with any narcotic or stimulant named. Europe takes only nine of those codes, each paired with its own substance: X42/X62/Y12 with a narcotic (T40.x), X41/X61/Y11 with a stimulant (T43.6), and X44/X64/Y14 with either. So the 74,525 slightly overcounts. The headline survives unless more than about 3,500 deaths drop out; three narrower WONDER exports will size it. The EUDA site blocks bots, so the code table came from a copy of an EMCDDA report hosted by Ireland's Health Research Board library.
   **Result (2026-11-14):** David ran the three narrower exports. Only 163 deaths dropped out (74,525 → 74,362), and the gap is still 13.1×. The wider code list had been almost harmless, because US coders put nearly every overdose under the nine codes Europe uses.
86. **A country with as many heavy meth users as the US has a tenth of its meth-positive dead pedestrians.** Splitting dead pedestrians into a stimulant bucket and a no-stimulant bucket per million people (`scripts/three_buckets.py`) put Czechia's stimulant bucket at about 0.43 a year in 2016–21, against 4.5 for the US in the same years: about 10×. Yet Czechia's heavy meth users (3,486 per million) are only about 1.3× fewer than the US's (4,619 per million, 2024). The buckets assume untested pedestrians look like tested ones, and Czechia's register counts blood results only and deaths within 24 hours, so they're estimates. Along the way the "49× meth deaths" figure turned out stale: it used 778 deaths in 7 EU countries, while the current 19-country count gives about 51× in the same year (2023), or 42× against the US 2024 rate.
87. **We wrote a bad "inconclusive" rule, and kept it.** P24 asked whether US pedestrians were hit more often or killed more easily when hit. It used NHTSA's injury-crash samples (GES until 2015, then its redesigned replacement CRSS). The pre-registered rule called a segment inconclusive if the change in pedestrians hit was within sampling error, and both segments were. But in 2016–24, deaths rose 19% while estimated hits fell about 8%. The "deadlier when hit" share's own 95% interval (72–219%) never got near the 50% line. The rule tested the wrong quantity. We scored it as written and reported the interval beside it. Two smaller snags: NHTSA's 2009–2014 file names (e.g. "GES09 FlatFile.zip", with a space) could only be found by listing the bucket, after guessed names returned 404s. And about 500–3,800 injured pedestrians a year have "severity unknown," a group our counts leave out, which is why they run slightly under NHTSA's published totals.

88. **Falling cocaine hid rising meth.** Counted together as "stimulants," dead pedestrians testing positive rose modestly from 2009 to 2014 (217 to 319 a year) and then jumped, so we dated the surge to 2015. Split by drug, meth/amphetamine more than tripled over those years (56 to 186), while cocaine fell from 230 in 2005 to 149 in 2014. The combined number masked the 2010 turn that Brumbelow found in meth alone. Lumping drugs into a class can hide opposite trends inside it.

89. **Which count of US heavy meth users you pick moves the US/Czechia ratio more than time does.** The "1.3×" used NSDUH's 2024 count of people with meth use disorder (4,620 per million) against Czechia's high-risk users (3,486 per million, 2022). RAND's count of chronic users (4+ days a month, built from arrestee urine tests) puts the US at 7,900–9,900 per million in 2013–16, against Czechia's 3,000–3,200 in the same years (NMC table 3-7): **2.6× in 2013, 3.3× in 2016**. On RAND's basis the US did pull away from Czechia, but the jump between methods (1.3× vs. about 3×) is bigger than the change over time. No single US series covers 2009–24: RAND stops in 2016, NSDUH changed its method in 2020, and Czech estimates start in 2013. RAND itself calls meth its least certain estimate (2015 range 1.4–5.5 million). The pedestrian tests rose much faster than users: meth-positive tested dead pedestrians per person went up about 6× from 2009 to 2016, while RAND's users per person went up about 1.8×. So the tests can't stand in for user counts. **Correction (2026-11-16):** the two counts aren't equals. We had already used RAND to size the survey's undercount (the survey misses homeless and incarcerated users), so RAND's about 3× (range 1.3–5.5×) is the best estimate and the survey's 1.3× is a known low reading.

91. **The "3%" for vehicle size had no paper trail.** Reel 3's card 24 says vehicle size is "3–10% of the rise". The 10% is the New York Times' hood-height model (about 300 deaths a year). The 3% was credited to Tyndall (2021), but no project file shows how it was derived, and the paper never states a share of the rise. Read directly (UHERO working paper 2020-4R, p. 26), Tyndall estimates that SUVs' growth from 13.6% to 25.7% of metro vehicles added about 128 pedestrian deaths a year by 2019 (99 in 2000 → 227), in metro areas holding 77% of the population. Against the national rise of about 2,160 a year from 2009 to 2019, that's about 6% as a raw count, or about 8% if metro deaths scale up with population (128 ÷ 0.77 ≈ 166). And it counts SUV growth since 2000, not since 2009, so the share of the post-2009 rise would be somewhat lower. The 3% looks like the paper's cumulative 1,100 deaths spread evenly over 19 years (about 58 a year), which understates the 2019 effect. A plausible honest range is about 5–10%. Every number that goes on screen needs its derivation written down, not just its source.

92. **The same expert dates the same waves three ways.** For Reel 4 (the four waves), we read Ciccarone's three papers side by side. In 2019, prescription-pill deaths rose "through 2016", and pill and heroin deaths had both "leveled off" in 2017 at about 15,000 each. In 2021, both "peaked in 2017". In the 2026 paper he co-wrote, pills turn down in 2017 but heroin in **2016**, a year earlier. Nobody changed their mind: the 2026 paper subtracts every death that also involved fentanyl from the pill and heroin lines, and by 2016 fentanyl was in a growing share of "heroin" deaths. The year 2011, often quoted as the end of the pill wave, is when *prescribing* peaked; pill deaths kept rising for five more years. Ciccarone also never gives the fourth wave a start year; "2015" is Friedman & Shover's (2023). Wave dates on screen need to say which definition they use.

93. **Taking fentanyl out changes when the waves peak and whether they cross.** Rebuilding the four waves from 16 CDC WONDER exports, with deaths that also involved fentanyl subtracted (the 2026 paper's method), changed two well-known "facts". Prescription-pill deaths didn't peak in 2016–17: they held flat at 10,400–10,900 a year from 2011 to 2016, with 2011 the single highest year (10,914). And heroin never overtook pills (10,304 vs. 10,664 at heroin's 2015 peak), although Ciccarone's 2019 chart, with fentanyl left in, has heroin passing pills in 2015. The rebuild matched every published check to the death (NCHS 2017 counts, the 2026 paper's 2023 split, Friedman & Shover's 235 and 34,429), except 2024, where final data moved one death from "fentanyl without a stimulant" (19,672) to "with" (28,063) against the paper's provisional 19,673 and 28,062.

95. **CDC's two provisional 2025 counts are 1,000 deaths apart.** WONDER's provisional database gives **69,645** overdose deaths for 2025 (queried Nov 19, 2026). CDC's provisional dashboard gave 69,973 in May and now reports 70,646.
   - The two products count differently. The dashboard uses 12-month-ending counts by the state where the death happened, and includes non-residents. WONDER counts US residents, by year of death.
   - WONDER's provisional database also reuses the 2024 population for 2025 and 2026, so its 2025 rates use the wrong denominator. That's a small error, since the population grows about 0.5% a year.
   - On the bright side, its 2024 numbers matched the final file in all 8 queries, so the two WONDER databases join cleanly.
   - For the chart, use WONDER's 69,645, label 2025 "provisional", and don't put the dashboard's ~70,600 next to it.
