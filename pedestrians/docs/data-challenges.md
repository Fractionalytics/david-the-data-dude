# Data challenges: an inside look

The data problems that surprised us, logged as they happened.

1. **The SUV boom came twice.** The story says sedans fell and SUVs rose. But in the government's sales data, pickups, SUVs and minivans already outsold cars in every year from 2001 to 2007, and pedestrian deaths stayed flat then (4,675–4,901 a year). The sedan collapse people remember (cars from 7.7M in 2014 to 2.7M in 2025) is a second wave, made of crossovers. Any chart has to show both waves, or the timing looks better than it is.
2. **Whose count of the dead?** IIHS and NHTSA both build their pedestrian counts from the same federal crash census, but they don't always match: 7,367 deaths in 2023 by IIHS's count, 7,314 in NHTSA's 2023 report. The crash files are revised after their first release, so the year you download matters.
3. **No one counts phones in crashes.** Smartphone ownership is easy to chart (35% of adults in 2011, 91% by 2024), but there's no reliable count of crashes where a phone was involved. The phone link can only be shown as timing.
4. **The world's road data is behind a membership.** The international database every country comparison uses (the ITF's IRTAD) offers only a selection free; the full annual series by country needs a membership. So the comparison rests on two years, 2013 and 2022, as published in a CDC study, rather than a line for every year.
5. **Europe got SUVs too.** The obvious explanation for America's rise in pedestrian deaths is SUVs. But SUVs went from 12% to 56% of new cars in Europe between 2010 and 2024, and European hoods got taller, while European pedestrian deaths fell. Whatever the US did differently, it wasn't simply buying SUVs.
6. **Exposure: the denominator nobody has.** A fair comparison would count deaths per mile walked, but no country measures walking well. Miles driven is the next best thing: US vehicle miles per person rose just 1.4% from 2013 to 2022, while pedestrian deaths per person rose 50%. For other countries the only comparable driving figures we found are from FHWA tables nearly 20 years old.
7. **No yearly ruler for hoods.** Europe has an average new-car hood height for every year (76.9 cm in 2010, 83.8 cm in 2024). The US has nothing like it in public: the best is a Consumer Reports analysis of industry data saying truck hoods rose at least 11% since 2000.
8. **The $3 isn't a price tag.** Advocates call the US pedestrian rule a "$3 fix". In NHTSA's own analysis, $3 isn't what a safer hood costs: its teardown found no visible difference in design or assembly, only a little extra weight, so the $2.86–$4.08 is the lifetime fuel cost of that weight.
9. **Doors that close on robots.** The Federal Register's website sends automated readers to a block page, the ITF's report returned 403, and the European DaCoTA project's site no longer exists. We used the government printing office's copy of the proposal (govinfo.gov), the Federal Register's own search API, and secondary summaries for the EU's early-2000s figures, and didn't work around any block.
10. **Two definitions of "truck".** EPA says 66% of 2024 model-year vehicles were trucks; the Commerce Department's sales data says 81% of 2024 sales. Both are right: EPA counts many small SUVs as cars, and one counts production by model year while the other counts sales by calendar year.
11. **The road map was redrawn in 2015.** The federal crash files describe each road by its "function" (interstate, arterial, collector, local), but the variable was replaced in 2015 with a new one using FHWA's newer road classes, and the old one had no code for rural freeways that aren't interstates. We built one mapping across both. Even so, the shift shows: deaths on local streets fell from 938 in 2015 to 633 in 2019 while arterial deaths rose from 3,121 to 4,000, partly because states were re-classifying their roads at the same time. Arterial's rise from 49% of deaths in 2000 to 62% in 2024 is real in direction, but a few points of it may be paperwork.
12. **"No sidewalk" really means "none noted".** The sidewalk field only starts in 2014, and its answers are "yes", "none noted" and "unknown". In 2024, 4,402 of 7,080 pedestrian deaths (62%) had no sidewalk noted, but that mixes roads with no sidewalk and reports that didn't record one.
13. **More drivers who were never found.** The striking vehicle's body type was unknown for about 6–9% of pedestrian deaths through 2019, then 12.4% in 2020 (811 deaths), and still 10.5% in 2024. Most are likely hit-and-runs, where no vehicle was ever inspected. Light-truck shares have to be quoted as a share of the deaths where the vehicle type is known.
14. **The newest year isn't settled.** The 2024 file NHTSA posts counts 7,080 pedestrian deaths. Crash files are revised after their first release (see 2), so 2024 may still move. Every earlier year we checked (2000, 2005, 2009, 2015, 2019, 2022, 2023) matched IIHS's published counts to within one death.
15. **Europe's total that isn't a total.** Eurostat publishes an "EU-27" pedestrian death count back to 1999, but it's just the sum of whichever countries reported that year. Lithuania is missing until 2012, Slovakia from 2011 to 2015, Malta from 2011 to 2014, and nine countries are missing in 2000. Only from 2016 are all 27 in. So we divide reporting countries' deaths by reporting countries' population, and note who's missing. The gaps are mostly high-death Eastern countries, so the early EU rate is, if anything, too low.
16. **Europe used to be worse.** In 2000 the EU's pedestrian death rate (2.29 per 100,000, even without nine Eastern members) was higher than America's (1.69). The lines crossed around 2010 (1.34 vs. 1.39). America wasn't always the outlier: Europe fell by two-thirds while America went up.
17. **Brexit broke the UK line.** Eurostat's UK series stops at 2018. From 2019 we use Britain's own Department for Transport counts, which cover Great Britain only, so Northern Ireland's roughly 16 pedestrian deaths a year are missing and the UK rate after 2018 is about 3% low.
18. **What counts as an SUV depends on who's counting.** For 2024, ICCT puts SUVs at 48% of new EU cars, JATO at 54% of Europe's, and T&E at 55.5% of the EU, UK and Norway's. For 2010 it's 9% (ICCT) against 11.5% (T&E). Each keeps its own list of which models are SUVs, and crossovers are where they disagree. We chart ICCT's because it's the only one with every year back to 2001, and call it the conservative count.
19. **Charts without tables.** The only yearly SUV series for the EU (ICCT) and the only yearly hood-height series (T&E) are published as charts with no numbers. We recovered ICCT's values from the drawing instructions inside its PDF, and read T&E's off the chart's pixels, to within about 0.1 cm. T&E's report says "click here for year-by-year data", but the link points back to the same page.
20. **An SUV in Europe is a car; in America it's a truck.** Europe files SUVs under "passenger cars", so its SUV share is a slice of cars. America's sales data puts SUVs, crossovers, pickups and minivans together as "light trucks" (81% of 2024 sales). So the two shares measure different things: the US one includes pickups and minivans, and Europe registers most pickups as vans, outside "cars" entirely.
21. **What counts as a pedestrian?** America's federal crash data puts people on wheelchairs, skateboards and scooters in their own category; the international databases count them as pedestrians. That's why the CDC's US rate for 2022 (2.33 per 100,000, 7,768 deaths) ran above ours (2.27): it adds about 250 "personal conveyance" deaths to a preliminary pedestrian count. With them added, our 2013 rate is exactly the CDC's 1.55. The US-vs-Europe charts now use the international definition.
22. **A category that vanished for two years.** In 2020 and 2021 only, the federal files split "person on a personal conveyance" into three codes (motorized, non-motorized, unknown), so a plain count of the old code showed zero such deaths in those years. Counting all three restores 182 and 214.
23. **Keeping the club the same.** Eurostat's "EU-27" pedestrian total in 2000 adds up only the 18 members that reported. Comparing 2000 with 2024 that way mixes a smaller club with a bigger one. We rebuilt the EU line from the 18 members that reported in every year from 2000 to 2024, and split it into Western members (before 2004) and Eastern ones. The headline holds: the EU fell from 2.29 to 0.75 per 100,000, and the US rose from 1.72 to 2.16.
24. **A drunk test that changed its decimal point.** The crash files record each pedestrian's blood alcohol, but in 2015 the unit switched from hundredths to thousandths. A reading of 80 meant 0.80 (a fatal level) before 2015 and the legal limit, 0.080, after. Testing also got thinner: 71% of pedestrians killed in 2009 had a test result, 57% in 2022, and in the newest file (2024) a third of results are simply "not reported". So we used NHTSA's own imputed alcohol files, which fill the gaps statistically, and checked that they match every real test result (they do, once you know NHTSA rounds 0.087 down to 0.08).
25. **Where the pedestrian stood, renumbered.** The "location" field was recoded in 2010, so the old and new codes don't line up one to one. Only three broad groups survive the break cleanly: at an intersection, mid-block on the road, and off the travel lanes. Even after 2010, "mid-block, crosswalk availability unknown" fell from 469 deaths to 197 while "mid-block, not in a crosswalk" rose, which looks like officers filling the field in more completely, not a change on the street.
26. **The speed limit moved from the crash to the car.** Through 2009 the files give one speed limit per crash; from 2010 they give the limit on the road each vehicle was on. We use the crash's limit through 2009 and the striking vehicle's after, and the share of deaths on roads posted 40 mph or more moves smoothly across the switch (61.8% in 2009, 62.6% in 2010).
27. **More drivers who left, more cars nobody saw.** Hit-and-runs rose from 16.6% of pedestrian deaths in 2009 to 24.4% in 2022, and they account for a third of the whole increase. That also means a growing share of deaths with no vehicle to describe: the striking vehicle's model year is unknown for 1,005 deaths in 2022, against 399 in 2009. Before 2009 the hit-and-run field had several "yes" codes we couldn't check against labels, so the series starts in 2009.
28. **The distraction field went quiet.** From 2010 the crash files say whether each driver was distracted, and by what. But for the drivers who hit and killed pedestrians, the answer "not reported" or "unknown" went from 19% of deaths in 2010-12 to 62% in 2020-22 ("not reported" alone went from 1,106 to 10,572 over the three years). The jump starts in 2016-17, which looks like states changing what their crash forms record, not drivers changing. Among drivers whose status is known, phone distraction rose from 1.2% to 2.0%, but that's only 42 phone-distracted drivers a year in 2010-12 and 54 in 2020-22, out of about 4,500 and 7,200 deaths a year. With most answers missing, the field can't say much about phones either way.
29. **The homelessness count is behind a bot wall.** HUD publishes its state-by-state homelessness counts as a spreadsheet, but its website answered every automated request with an Amazon "challenge" page instead of the file. We didn't try to get around it, so the state homelessness test waits for someone to download the file by hand.
30. **The Census door now wants a key.** To test whether deaths grew faster in poorer or car-free neighborhoods, we needed each census tract's poverty and car ownership from the Census Bureau's data API. That API used to answer small requests without signing up; this time it returned a "Missing Key" page instead of data, and even its robots.txt came back "Request Rejected". The tract maps themselves downloaded fine, and 98.7–99.7% of pedestrian deaths in 2009–11 and 2020–22 carry coordinates that land in a 2010 tract (25–54 deaths a year have none). The neighborhood tests wait on a free API key.
31. **A hurricane in the baseline.** To test whether homelessness explains where pedestrian deaths rose, we compared each state's unsheltered homeless count in 2009 with 2022. But 2009 wasn't a normal year everywhere: Louisiana's count was still swollen four years after Hurricane Katrina, and Florida's by the recession, so both show big "drops" in unsheltered homelessness (−160 and −128 per 100,000) that mostly reflect an unusual starting point. The workbook also changed its column names: older editions label them "Unsheltered Homeless, 2009", this one just "Unsheltered Homeless" on a sheet per year, which broke our first reading of it.
32. **A third of America has no bus riders, on paper.** To test whether transit riders' neighborhoods saw bigger rises, we split census tracts into five equal-population groups by the share of workers who commute by public transportation. But 29% of Americans live in a tract where the Census survey found no transit commuters at all, so the whole bottom fifth, and part of the second, is tracts tied at exactly zero. Which zero-tracts land in the bottom fifth then depends on how ties are broken; our rule breaks them by tract number, which puts low-numbered states first. Breaking the ties at random instead gave the same verdict. The night-shift measure has the opposite problem: it is a small share everywhere (the top fifth starts at 6.7% of workers leaving home between midnight and 5am, the bottom fifth tops out at 1.5%), so in a small tract it rests on a handful of survey answers.
33. **Two official overdose tables, and neither covers both decades.** CDC's downloadable state table of age-adjusted overdose death rates stops in 2016; its newer state map covers 1999, 2005 and 2014–2024, skipping 2009–2011. So the overdose test stitches the two together (2009–11 from one, 2020–22 from the other). Where they overlap, 2015 and 2016 agree to within 0.05 per 100,000, but the map's 2014 figures for Arkansas and Arizona look swapped (18.2 and 12.6, against 12.6 and 18.2 in the table), and some small states show a rate of 0 in 1999 and 2005 where the number was withheld. None of those years enter the test.
34. **The drug codes changed four years after the "overhaul".** The crash files' drug-test results were reorganized in 2018: instead of at most three results per person, a separate file lists every result (up to 33 for one pedestrian in 2024) and which sample was tested. But the drug codes themselves stayed the same until 2022, when they switched to a new numbering (fentanyl went from 151 to 1059) with hundreds of newly named substances, including caffeine and nicotine filed under "stimulants". Counting the whole stimulant class would have made 2022 jump for a reason that has nothing to do with meth or cocaine, so we matched the named drugs across both schemes by hand. Testing also thinned: 60% of pedestrians killed in 2009–11 had a valid drug result, 56% in 2020–22 and 49% in 2024.
35. **No meth count by state before 2015, and most states missing in 2015.** We wanted each state's methamphetamine overdose rate for 2009–11. Outside CDC WONDER (which needs terms accepted by hand), CDC publishes psychostimulant deaths for that period only for the nation as a whole. Its state-by-drug counts start in 2015, so the test fell back to 2015 vs. 2022. And in 2015, CDC withheld the meth count for 27 states because too few of their death certificates named the drug, including California, Texas, Florida and Arizona, where meth matters most. Three more (DC, New Hampshire, Rhode Island) had fewer than 10 deaths and were withheld. Louisiana has no drug-specific rows at all in any year. That left 21 states, and CDC's own notes say state comparisons of drug-specific rates "should not be made".
36. **New York is two places in the overdose file.** CDC's provisional overdose table lists "New York" without New York City, and New York City as its own row. Read naively, New York's 2015 overdose total would be 1,802 instead of 2,761. We add the two back together. Vermont, meanwhile, shows exactly 0 meth deaths in 2015 with no suppression note, where other small counts are hidden.
37. **The "old cars" number was the whole fleet aging.** Deaths involving vehicles 15 or more years old went from 471 to 1,863 a year, which looked like a lead. But the share of old vehicles roughly doubled in *every* fatal crash, not just pedestrian ones: 16.9% → 34.3% of vehicles in crashes with no pedestrian, vs. 14.2% → 27.5% among vehicles that hit one. After the 2008 recession Americans kept cars longer (mean age in those crashes 9.1 → 11.2 years). Only a control group caught it.
38. **Fleeing drivers hide their license status.** A driver who hits a pedestrian and drives away has no known license status, and hit-and-run grew from 17.5% to 23.7% of pedestrian deaths. So license status is unknown for 16% of pedestrian deaths in 2020–22, against 2% of other fatal crashes. The not-licensed share we can measure (8.7% → 12.7%) rose no faster than in other crashes, but the drivers most likely to be unlicensed are exactly the ones we can't see.
39. **The last census on old tract lines is 2015–19.** Measuring tract population growth needs the same tract boundaries at both ends, and census tracts were redrawn for 2020. The last five-year survey on 2010 boundaries is 2015–19, so the "after" population is about three years older than the 2020–22 deaths it's divided into. Twenty-five tract codes in 2015–19 don't appear in 2008–12 (and 26 the other way), corrections the Census Bureau made between releases; they're left out.
40. **"Outside any city" shrank, and it isn't because people moved.** FARS gives each crash a city code, with 0 meaning outside a city. The share of fatal crashes coded 0 fell from 55% in 2009 to 51% in 2015 and 47% in 2022, while the number of distinct city codes rose from 2,079 to 2,231. Cities annex land and states change how they code, so a city's death count can grow just because its borders did. For the city tests we place crashes inside fixed 2010 city boundaries using the crash coordinates instead.
41. **Whether a dead pedestrian gets a drug test depends on the state.** In FARS, the share of pedestrians killed with a drug result ranges from about 3% in North Carolina to 96% in Nevada (per the meth evidence review). A national "share positive" mixes states that test almost everyone with states that test almost no one, so it can move just because the mix changes. Only four states tested at least 70% every year from 2009 to 2022 (Maryland, New Jersey, Washington, West Virginia). In those four, the stimulant share still rose, from about 8% to about 21%.
42. **Two official numbers for Germany's pedestrian deaths.** Eurostat lists 375 pedestrians killed in Germany in 2022; reports based on Germany's own statistics office give about 366–368. Same country, same year, two government sources, about 2% apart. The trend we use (591 in 2009 to under 400) doesn't change, but it's a reminder that "the official number" depends on which office you ask.
43. **"After dark" can mean two things.** Measured by the clock (6pm to 6am), 84% of the rise in US pedestrian deaths from 2009 to 2022 happened after dark. Measured by what the police report says about the light (dark, daylight, dawn, dusk), it's 87%. In summer, 6 to 9pm is still light, so the clock version undercounts. And "daylight deaths stayed flat" is true from 2000 (1,671 a year) to 2024 (1,653), but not from 2009, a low year (1,241), after which they rose 35%.
